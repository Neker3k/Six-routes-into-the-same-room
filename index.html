One room, six ways in

The film follows the people behind the gear and asks what brings them to the same community.

A room full of different beginnings

At a gathering in Astana, people arrived in helmets, masks, camouflage and carefully assembled outfits. Some carried airsoft replicas; others sat nearby with paper and pencils. Local artists drew the participants as they talked, posed and waited for their portraits. For a moment, the room held two versions of the same group: the people in front of the artists, and the people taking shape on the page.

The gathering offers a way into a community that can be easy to misread from a distance. The equipment is immediately visible. The reasons people give for being there take longer to hear. Six participants describe different routes into airsoft and military cosplay: childhood interests, video games, family influence, a search for self-development, and the chance to make a character or an outfit their own. Across those accounts, another theme keeps returning: friends who become a team, and a place where people can share an interest without having to explain it from the beginning.

This is not a survey of every airsoft player in Astana. It is a portrait of six people interviewed at one gathering. Their accounts do not add up to a single definition of the hobby. They show what the same room can mean to people who arrived there by different paths.

Some interests begin early

For Salt, the interest had been there for years before she joined the community. She says she has been involved in the military-themed scene for nearly a year, and that building her own M4 replica was part of making a long-held wish real. She also describes the people she met as close friends—brothers and sisters she can rely on. When she talks about airsoft, she is candid about the sting of being hit by a pellet. The discomfort is real, she says, but it becomes familiar. Her answer moves quickly from equipment to relationships: what she values most is not just what she wears, but who is beside her.

Another participant traces his interest to childhood. At five, he remembers playing Call of Duty: Modern Warfare at his uncle’s home. Later, at Comic Con in 2023, he saw people in military-inspired costumes and was struck by the way they had built their looks. He first came to a convention in a different cosplay; the next year he wore a Ghost costume. The mask, he recalls, changed how he felt in the room. People approached for photographs, and the costume gave him a way to relax, express himself and make something creative. Airsoft came later, after years of wanting to try it. He also notes the cost: for him, playing regularly depends on being able to save for it.

Tachanka’s path runs through cosplay and family history. He says he began military-themed cosplay in 2015 with a character from Rainbow Six. He grew up liking military clothing and style, and says several members of his family were in the military. Airsoft, he explains, gave him a team of friends to play and talk with. His description is direct: the game brings people and their hobbies together, and lets them share memorable moments.

For Gator, the distinction between a personal interest and a formal military path is especially clear. Both of his parents served in the military, and after school he entered a military academy. He left because he did not like the experience there. He already knew about airsoft and military cosplay, however, and found a way to continue exploring the subject on his own terms. Today, he describes the hobby as something personal that still gives him room to develop and keep learning.

The same clothes, different meanings

Gasyr describes a route shaped by a change in direction. He first pursued a military path through an academy, then moved on when it was not right for him. He says the interest continued through physical development, self-defence and the friendships he found at events. His account resists a simple story of either staying in or leaving a military environment: the hobby let him keep exploring parts of that interest outside the academy.

Baton says he began with a general fascination with military themes, then gradually assembled outfits connected to different games. He has been involved for about four years. His account makes the process sound incremental: one piece of equipment leads to another look, and the looks grow alongside the games and characters he enjoys. The costume is not a uniform handed down as a finished identity. It is something he builds, adjusts and associates with his own interests.

The interviews use words such as “military,” “cosplay” and “airsoft” in overlapping ways, but the participants do not all mean the same thing by them. For some, the draw is the game and its physical intensity. For others, it is the craft of assembling a look, inhabiting a character, or finding a social circle. Several do more than one of these things. The distinctions matter: describing everyone simply as a player would miss the creative work, while describing everyone only as a cosplayer would miss the sport and team play.

There are also limits and costs. Airsoft can hurt, and one participant calls it expensive. Another left a military academy after deciding it was not for him. These details complicate a neat image of enthusiasm. They show people choosing which parts of a military-inspired hobby fit their lives, rather than following one common route.

A team is made of ordinary time together

The most consistent thread is not a piece of equipment. It is companionship. Salt talks about close friends she can rely on. Tachanka talks about a team with whom he can play and discuss different things. Gator describes finding a place to keep developing an interest after leaving the academy. A participant who came through cosplay says the group helped him continue growing and introduced him to people with similar interests.

That does not mean the community is identical for everyone. The interviews point to different motivations and different levels of commitment. One person speaks about the emotional release of a costume; another emphasizes physical development; another talks about the friendships formed around the game. The shared space holds these differences without making them disappear. That is part of what the gathering makes visible: people can arrive for separate reasons and still spend the day together.

The artists add another layer to the gathering. Their sketches take the silhouettes, poses and details that usually signal a character and reduce them to pencil lines. The drawings are not evidence of what the artists think about the community; their own perspective would need to be heard in an interview. Here, the sketches show a moment of exchange: participants offer a pose, artists interpret it, and the group watches the image take shape.

What holds the group together?

The six interviews do not settle on one answer—and they do not need to. They offer a set of different beginnings: a long-standing interest, a video game remembered from childhood, a cosplay discovered at a convention, a family connection, an interrupted academy path, and the gradual building of an outfit. The reasons for staying are similarly varied, but friendship appears again and again.

Seen from the outside, the room can look like a collection of gear. Heard from the people inside it, it becomes a network of interests, decisions and relationships. The gathering with local artists makes that contrast tangible. Participants arrive wearing versions of characters and styles they have chosen; the artists draw those choices; and the interviews explain what the costumes alone cannot.

This portrait is limited to six voices and one event. It does not claim to speak for every player or cosplayer in Astana. It asks a smaller question: what can be seen when a community is described by the people who make it up? In these interviews, the answer begins with personal interests—but it keeps returning to the friends made along the way.
